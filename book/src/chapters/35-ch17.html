Chapter 17

Running the Five Phases

Running the Score (Phase 1)

Set the scene. Put the map on the table and describe the location from a spider’s eye — the geography, the obvious features, the objective, the threats in plain sight. Then stop. Don’t inventory the hidden dangers. Let the crew ask questions, and answer honestly where a spider could reasonably know the answer. Decline where they couldn’t. Ten to fifteen minutes; the crew will be itching to move.

Running Planning (Phase 2)

This is where the crew earns what they don’t already know. When they take Casing rolls, have your hidden intel ready and hand out one true detail per Success — the cat’s route, the type of lock, the loose vent, the hazard by the sink. Give them real information; it’s not a trick. But hold the line between knowing and winning — casing tells the crew the cat naps until two; it doesn’t make the cat sleep in.

As the crew makes Preparations, note each one and quietly assign it a complication — not one that cancels the prep, one that adds texture:

  • “The cat’s nap schedule is solid” — but the human fed it early today, so it wakes ten minutes ahead.
  • “The tripwire is set” — but a stray candy wrapper is caught against it and will crackle the moment it’s disturbed.
  • “The window is our exit” — and it is. There’s also a moth on the outside of the glass right now, and moths are dramatic.

The prep works. The world is just complicated.

Running the Heist (Phase 3)

The crew moves across the map. Update the Alert visibly every time it changes. Describe each obstacle as the crew reaches it, give them a beat to decide their approach, then let them commit. When the Alert crosses a threshold, announce the change immediately — the moment it hits 5 and the cat gets up, say so. The crew adjusts, and that adjustment is the game.

Running the Escape (Phase 4)

Raise the energy. Keep it short and physical: “The cat’s behind you and it’s fast. The window is eight squares out. What do you do?” This is not the phase for long deliberation — it’s silk, instinct, and fast decisions. And hold yourself to the rule: at least one escape obstacle must be solvable without Athletics. Frame obstacles with several answers — a staffer in the hallway invites Athletics (push past), Deception (talk through), Stealth (wait for the gap), or Acrobatics (over the shelf). If you’ve called for Athletics three times running, you’ve broken the rule. Reframe the next one.

Running the Debrief (Phase 5)

Let the crew breathe. Ask what they think went wrong. Let them argue. Award AP. Ask if anyone wants to describe what their spider does in the immediate aftermath. Then: “There’s another job. Interested?” They’re always interested.
