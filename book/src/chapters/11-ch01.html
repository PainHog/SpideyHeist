Chapter 1

Welcome to Heisty Spideys

It began, as most great crimes do, with opportunity. The cookie tin was unguarded. The human was asleep. The cat was a problem, but the crew had a plan for the cat. The plan did not work. They rarely do. That has never once stopped anyone.

You are a spider.

You live in the walls of a building owned by creatures ten thousand times your size, who would respond to noticing you with anything from a startled noise to a rolled-up magazine. By any reasonable measure you are small and in danger.

You are also a criminal mastermind.

You have watched the humans for weeks. You know the schedule. You know where the good things are kept. You know the cat naps from two to four, and that the sensor in the hall has a blind spot exactly one spider wide. You have a crew. You have a plan. The plan is held together with silk and optimism, but it is a plan.

Tonight you run it. Something will go wrong — possibly everything. You will succeed anyway, or fail spectacularly, or, most likely, both, in a way the crew will discuss for the rest of your short and eventful careers.

Welcome to Heisty Spideys.

What This Game Is

Heisty Spideys is a tabletop roleplaying game played on a grid. You need a map marked in squares, a token for each spider, dice, and a group of friends willing to commit small crimes as small animals. One of you runs the world. The rest play spiders stealing something out of it.

You can play it on a printed map with tokens and a handful of dice, or on a virtual tabletop — a companion game system for Foundry VTT is being released alongside this book. Every rule here is built to work the same way in both places: count the squares, move the tokens, roll the dice.

The inspiration is heist stories. Ocean’s Eleven. The Italian Job. Leverage. The episode of any show where the team breaks into somewhere fancy and it all goes beautifully wrong. If you have ever watched one of those and thought I would like to do that, but as a spider, this book was written for you specifically.

The tone is comedic disaster with real stakes. Things go wrong. The Alert climbs. Someone sneezes at the exact wrong moment. But the chance of actually failing is real, and the tension is what makes the comedy land — a near-miss isn’t funny unless there was something to miss.

The Storyteller

One player runs the world. They are the Storyteller, or ST, because writing it out every time is a job.

The Storyteller’s job is not to beat the crew. The ST builds a place that is interesting to fail inside — a location with real geography, active threats, a cat with opinions, and at least one thing the crew didn’t account for. The ST runs that world honestly and lets it do the work. The world catches spiders on its own. It doesn’t need help.

Full guidance is in Part Two. Read Part One first anyway. Knowing what the crew can do makes you a sharper ST.

The Crew

Everyone else plays a spider with a job. Your spider has a Species (what kind of spider you are, which grants a passive ability), a Crew Role (your function on the job), Attributes and Skills (your numbers), a Speed (how far you move on the grid), and exactly one Flaw that will surface at the worst available time.

No two spiders are alike. The Ghost reaches places nobody else can. The Tinkerer builds tools out of whatever’s on the floor. The Face can talk a house cat into looking the other way. The Bruiser cannot, and has stopped trying. The Bruiser has other methods.

The Waiting Web — Nobody Sits Out

If your spider is caught — jarred, vacuumed, scooped up by a delighted child — you are not out of the game. You draw from the Waiting Web: a backup spider who was outside the whole time, got the distress signal, and is on their way in.

Your replacement arrives at the next obstacle, a little worse for having heard what happened to the last one, but in the job. The game doesn’t stop for casualties. It barely slows down. The best heist stories don’t end when someone gets caught. They get worse, which is better.

What You Need

  • 2–6 players, one of them the Storyteller.
  • A pile of six-sided dice. At least six per player, more is better. Actual play uses only d6s. (The character-creation tables in Chapter 20 use a d10 and a pair of d6s — that’s for rolling up a spider, not for the heist.)
  • A grid map for each heist — a printed battle map, a dry-erase grid, or a virtual tabletop.
  • A token per spider, plus tokens for the cat, the human, and anything else that moves.
  • Something to track Silk Points per spider — coins, beads, bits of gravel.
  • A shared Alert track everyone can see — a whiteboard number, a row of dice, a line on paper.
  • Snacks. Heists take time.
