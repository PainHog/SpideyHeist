Chapter 3

The Grid — Tiles, Tokens, and Movement

Heisty Spideys is played on a square grid. Every heist location is a map marked in squares; every spider, cat, human, and vacuum is a token that stands on one. When something moves, you move the token and count the squares. This is what keeps a heist honest — everyone can see exactly how far the cat is, exactly how far you have to go, and exactly how bad the timing is.

You can run this on a printed map, a dry-erase grid, or a virtual tabletop. The rules don’t change. Count squares, move tokens, roll dice.

One Square = One Spider

A single grid square is about the size of one spider. Your token fills one square. When a rule gives a distance, it’s measured in squares.

Distances are never inches or “spider-lengths” — always squares. A reach of “6 squares” is most of the way across a small room, which, to a spider, is a long way.

Speed

Your Speed is how many squares you can move on your turn. It’s set by your species (Chapter 4). Most spiders have Speed 5. Wolf Spiders run at 7, Jumping Spiders at 6. Crab Spiders, who would rather wait, move 4.

  • Diagonal moves count as one square. Keep it simple; a step is a step.
  • Being Hurt or Critical cuts your Speed in half (round down). See Chapter 10.

Your Turn: Move + One Action

On your turn you may move up to your Speed and take one Action. You can split your movement — move a few squares, act, move the rest. You don’t have to use all of it, or any of it.

An Action is the thing you roll for, or a deliberate equivalent: pick a lock, make a Stealth check, shove a door, spit silk, Assist an ally, patch a crewmate, use a Signature Move (unless it says otherwise). You get one per turn.

Free actions don’t cost your Action: a quick word to the crew, dropping an item, glancing around. If you’re not sure whether something is free, it probably takes your Action.

Example Turn

Nook the Ghost is 4 squares from a cracked-open drawer, with the cat’s token 6 squares away and asleep. Nook has Speed 5. She moves 4 squares to the drawer (movement), then picks the lock (Action: Engineering). She has one square of movement left but stays put — no reason to walk back into the open.

Adjacency and Reach

The eight squares around you — orthogonal and diagonal — are adjacent. Anything you do up close (a Brawl, an Assist, patching a crewmate, grabbing the loot from someone’s grip) needs you to be adjacent, unless a rule says otherwise.

Ranged reach — the Spitting Spider’s strand, a thrown distraction, a silk grapple — is measured in squares along the shortest path, and needs line of sight to the target.

Line of Sight and Cover

Line of sight is a straight line from your square to another that isn’t fully blocked by a wall or solid object. You can’t spot, target, or be spotted through a wall. This cuts both ways, constantly, and learning to use it is most of playing well.

Cover: if a solid object sits between you and something that might see you — a mug, a book, a table leg — you have cover. Increase the Difficulty to spot or target you by 2. A spider that keeps furniture between itself and the cat is a spider the cat has trouble finding.

Climbing, Squeezing, and Rough Ground

You are a spider. The walls and ceiling are yours.

  • Climbing: moving up a wall or across a ceiling costs normal movement. Spiders climb; that’s the whole point of being one.
  • Slick or hostile surfaces: freshly cleaned glass, a moving appliance, anything actively trying to shed you costs double movement, or an Acrobatics roll if the ST calls for one.
  • Rough ground: a cluttered or hazard-edged square costs +1 movement to enter.
  • Squeezing: a gap smaller than a square takes an Acrobatics roll (Difficulty 2) to pass. Cellar Spiders ignore this entirely.
  • Occupied squares: you can’t end your turn in a square another creature occupies. You may pass through an ally’s square. You may not pass through an enemy’s — except a Cellar Spider, who folds around anything.

Turn Order

Most of a heist doesn’t need turns at all — the crew moves and acts, the ST responds, everyone keeps up. Turn order only matters when timing is tight: an active cat, a chase, a room where seconds count.

When it matters, it’s simple. The crew takes its turns first, in whatever order the crew agrees on, then the ST runs the threats. Repeat until the moment is over. No initiative rolls, no tracking who’s on what count. The crew coordinates because coordinating is the fun part; the world answers all at once, because the world is not on the crew’s schedule.

Storyteller Note — Keep the Map Honest

The grid is a promise: what players can see is true. If the cat is 6 squares away, it is 6 squares away, and a player who counts is allowed to count. Don’t fudge distances to manufacture tension — the honest distances are tense enough. When you place a threat, place it where it actually is, and let the crew do the math. Half of this game is a spider staring at a token going we can make that. Probably. We can probably make that.
